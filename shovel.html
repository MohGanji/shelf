We just invented the shovel

Man is a tool-using animal. Without tools he is nothing, with tools he is all.

– Thomas Carlyle

It’s 1750.

The sun is barely up and you’re already in the trench, shoveling dirt into a wheelbarrow.

When the shovel hits rock, you trade it for a pickaxe and swing until the rock gives.

Every stone in the wall behind you, you cut by hand.

Every barrow of earth, you push up a plank and tip out, then come back for the next.

Your back is the engine. Your hands are the machine.

A house will take you a season.

The cathedral down the road has been going up since before you were born, and it will still be going up after you’re gone.

This is the whole world of building. You, a few tools, and your own back.

Building software used to look exactly like this.

Until very recently, we wrote every line of it by hand.

And like anyone working by hand, we found the moves that worked and we kept them.

A loop, to do a thing again and again.

A function, to name a handful of steps and reach for them later.

A data structure, to hold things in a shape that made sense.

An algorithm, a route through a problem that someone had already walked.

These were our primitives.

Small, intuitive pieces, worn smooth from use.

We captured them, gave them names, taught them to whoever came next, and passed them around until everyone had them.

Every piece of software ever shipped was built out of these same few pieces, by hand, one line at a time.

Then someone built a machine.

The shovel became an excavator.

The pickaxe became a jackhammer.

The wheelbarrow became a dump truck.

The man dragging stone across a plank became a crane setting it ten floors up in one breath.

None of the ideas changed. You still dig, you still break, you still carry.

But what a single person could move went up a thousandfold, overnight.

And when that happens, the question stops being “how do we build this faster.”

It becomes “what could we build now that we never could before?”

The cathedral that once ate a lifetime now goes up in a year, and taller.

We are standing at that exact moment, except the machine is the agent.

And here is the strange, wonderful part.

We are building the machines of this new era by rediscovering the same primitives we already knew by hand.

A skill is a function. Something you define once, name, and call again and again.

A routine is a loop. The same work, run until it’s done.

A workflow is a graph. Primitives wired to primitives, one feeding into the next.

“So it’s the old stuff with new names?”

Yes. That’s exactly the point.

What the full set will look like, I don’t know yet.

But I know what happens to primitives once they settle.

They get taught in schools. They get asked in interviews.

They get mixed and matched, wrapped inside one another, nested and combined, until people build whole systems out of them without a second thought.

The way we stopped noticing the while loop.

Now stand in a city, a hundred years after that first machine.

Before the machines, we had houses. Dirt roads. A stone bridge, if the town was lucky.

After them, we had skyscrapers. Highways drawn across whole countries. Subways carved out beneath entire cities.

Same act. Dig, break, carry.

But nobody looks up at a skyscraper anymore. It’s just the skyline.

Every piece of software we have ever built is a house. A gravel road. A stone bridge.

Careful, handmade, one line at a time.

And we have just built the machine.

So picture the digital city, a hundred years from now.

We already know what the houses look like.

What do the skyscrapers look like? The highways? The subways running underneath it all?
